Twelve Articles, Thirty-Seven Tags: Auditing a Writing Section Against the Research

A personal writing section usually grows one article at a time, and each article is judged on its own words rather than on how a reader moves through the collection. This article explores what happened when the writing section of this site was audited as a whole, against published research on how people read long pages, and then changed to close the gaps. The work was carried out by an AI coding agent from a written brief, and each change was reviewed before it went live on 15 November 2026. The article is organised as follows: first, why the reader who arrives from a link matters most; second, what the audit measured; third, what changed, with screenshots taken before and after; fourth, how the change was tested; and finally, the limits of what can be claimed.

This article does not argue that these changes bring more readers. Rather, it claims that a writing section can be measured against stated guidance in the same way as any other product, and that the gaps it reveals are concrete enough to fix and to test.

1. Why the reader from a link matters

The articles on this site are shared through LinkedIn posts and found through search, so a reader is likely to land in the middle of one article rather than on the home page. According to Nielsen (1997), most people scan a web page rather than read it word by word; in other words, a long article has to show its structure before it can expect to be read. It follows that the article page, not the home page, is where navigation has to work.

The research reviewed for this work gives fairly concrete guidance, and it can be summarised briefly. Nielsen Norman Group describes a table of contents as an ordered list of linked headings. According to the same guide, such a list gives a scannable overview, direct access to each section and better discovery of content low on the page (Wang and Brown, 2023). In the same guide, a list placed in the main body is the placement that carries most easily to a phone, whereas a sticky, collapsed list on mobile was often not noticed in testing. For pages longer than about four screens, Loranger (2017) recommends one "Back to Top" button, labelled in words, in the lower right. Finally, Baymard Institute recommends a line length of roughly 50 to 75 characters for comfortable reading.

2. What the audit measured

The audit applied that guidance to all twelve articles, and three findings stood out. First, there was no way to navigate a long article. The longest, a guide to real API traffic in Playwright Java, runs to about 6,400 words under 35 headings, yet it had neither a contents list nor links to its headings. Second, every article ended in a dead end: six closed with a small "More articles" line and six with nothing at all. Third, the tags were too scattered to act as navigation, since 37 different tags were spread across twelve articles and 31 of them were used only once.

Interestingly, the audit also confirmed something that was already right: the line length measured about 55 characters, inside the recommended range, so the reading column itself was left unchanged.

3. What changed

Each change answers one finding, and each was kept as small as the finding allowed.

Topics instead of tags. When the change went live, the archive gained one row of topic buttons with counts: All (12), AI agents (4), LLMs and RAG (3), Testing (3) and Self-hosting (2). The distinction matters: a tag describes one article, whereas a topic groups several. For a collection this small, a single filter appears more useful than a faceted search (Nielsen Norman Group, filters versus facets), and the tags remain on each row as description.

Figure 1: The archive before and after: the same list, with one row of topics and counts above it.

A contents list on every article. At first, only the eight articles with at least five sections and 1,500 words received an "On this page" list in the main text, on the reasoning that a list of three headings adds length without adding direction. However, that left readers seeing a list on some articles and not on others, so a follow-up change gave it to every article with three or more sections, which is all twelve. In addition, every heading can now be linked to directly.

Figure 2: The top of the longest article: the reader now sees nine sections before the first paragraph.

An ending that leads somewhere. Every article now closes with "Keep reading": the next newer and older essays, and a link to the full list. Moreover, a labelled "Back to top" button appears in the lower right once the reader has scrolled, and code blocks in the step-by-step guides have a copy button.

Figure 3: The end of an article: before, the page ran straight into the footer; after, it offers the next essay.

4. How the change was tested

The new pieces were covered by an automated suite: 206 tests were reported passing before the change was merged, and 208 after the follow-up, which added a test that no article is left without its contents list. The more useful evidence, however, came from a check written for an earlier piece of work. The contrast audit described in an earlier article measures every piece of text against the background it actually sits on. It flagged the new archive rows: in the dark theme, the small date line fell just under 7:1 where it sat over the gold glow of the page background, and the colour was therefore corrected before release. In other words, a test written for one change caught a regression in the next, which is precisely what such a test is for.

Limits

Several limits should be kept in view, and the first is that the site has no analytics, so nothing here shows that readers now go further; the claim is conformance to guidance, not a measured effect. Second, the research is general guidance on long web pages, not a study of personal technical blogs. Third, each article was assigned to its topic from its title and tags, and at least one assignment is a judgement call. For example, the crypto signal engine sits under Testing because its argument rests on tests that prove it cannot trade. Finally, the screenshots were rendered from the site's own build, before and after the change, in the dark theme at desktop width.

Summary and conclusion

This article set out to show that a writing section can be audited like a product. The main finding is that the gaps were structural rather than textual: long articles had no map, every article ended in a dead end, and the tags had grown too scattered to guide anyone. Taken together, the changes give the reader who arrives from a link three things the old pages lacked: a view of the whole article before the first paragraph, a way back to the top, and a next step at the end. It can therefore be said that the method matters more than any single feature: state the guidance, measure the pages against it, change only what the measurement shows, and keep the earlier tests running, because they will catch what the new work breaks.

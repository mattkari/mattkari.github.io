How I let an AI agent file my dental insurance claim (without giving it my password)

Matt Karimov · Test automation engineer · October 2026

My wife needed a root canal. Our workplace dental plan covers part of it, but only if someone sits down with the insurer's claim portal, splits the dentist's statement into the right benefit categories, uploads the evidence and gets it all in before the deadline. Nobody in our house was looking forward to that job.

So I handed it to the AI agent I run at home. It did most of the work, and it left me a saved draft where all I had to do was enter my bank details and press Submit. This guide covers how I set that up, what went wrong along the way, and the rules I'd keep next time. I've left out the insurer's name and any personal details. Most claim portals work in a similar way, so the steps should carry over.

What you need

  • An agent that can drive a real browser. I use a self-hosted agent on a Raspberry Pi with a browser automation tool. Any agent that can open pages, read the DOM, fill fields and upload files will do.
  • A password vault the agent can use but can't read. This is the important part, and step 2 covers it.
  • The paperwork: your policy terms (the PDF with the "what is covered / not covered" tables), the itemised statement from the dentist, and any supporting letter.
  • About 30 minutes of your attention, mostly at the start and at the end.

Step 1: Let the agent read the policy before it touches the portal

Give the agent the policy terms and the dentist's statement first, and ask for a written plan before it does anything: which statement line goes under which benefit, at what percentage, against how much of each annual limit you have left, and what refund to expect.

For us, the plan put the first appointment under the emergency benefit (paid at 100%, because my wife was in pain and it hadn't been booked in advance) and the rest of the root canal under routine and restorative (paid at 80%, up to what was left of her annual limit). Having the expected refund written down at the start gave me something to check the insurer's eventual payout against.

The emergency benefit needed proof that the visit really was an emergency, and the dentist's statement didn't say so. I asked the agent to draft a short, polite email to the practice requesting written confirmation, which I sent myself. The dentist replied the same evening. Sort out missing evidence before you start the claim, not halfway through it.

Step 2: Put the login in a vault, never in the chat

This is the rule I care about most. Never type your password into the conversation with your agent. Chat history gets logged, summarised, sent to model providers and kept for a long time.

My agent has a local encrypted vault. I add a login on the Pi's terminal, and the password goes into a hidden prompt:

hermes vault add --kind login
Label: Insurer
Site origin: https://www.example-insurer.co.uk
Identifier type (email/phone/username) [email]:   <- just press Enter
Identifier: me@example.com
Password: ********

The agent only ever sees a handle such as vault_a1b2… and the username. When it reaches the login page, it types the username itself and asks the vault to fill the password field. The vault checks the page's origin first and won't fill the password on any other site. The password never shows up in the model's context.

Two things caught me out:

  • "Identifier type" means the kind of identifier, not the value. I typed my email address there four times before I worked that out. The prompt wants email, or just Enter.
  • Use the bare origin, not the full login URL. https://www.example-insurer.co.uk, not …/employees/default.aspx. The vault matches on the origin.

Step 3: Expect the login to fight back a bit

The first time the agent asked the vault to fill the password, it came back with "no fillable login field matched". The portal had marked its login boxes as autocomplete="new-password", which made them look like a sign-up form. The agent changed the attributes on the page to username and current-password, and the fill then worked. All this changes is the label the browser uses to classify the field. It doesn't get around any security, and the server sees an ordinary login.

The next attempt failed with "we cannot match the details you have entered". I'd changed my password on the website but hadn't updated the vault. Rule: two failed logins and the agent stops. A third could lock the account. It told me what it thought was wrong and waited. I logged in by hand on my phone to confirm the new password, saved it in the vault again, and the next attempt worked first time.

Step 4: Enter one statement line per claim line, using the statement's own wording

The portal wanted every treatment as a separate line: name, type (routine, injury or emergency), NHS or private, date, amount. The agent typed in each line from the dentist's statement. Some points I'd recommend:

  • Use the statement's exact names ("Endodontic exploratory appointment fee", not "root canal assessment"). The person assessing the claim compares your lines with the receipt, and identical wording makes that quick.
  • Show discounts in the line itself. Our root canal was £687 with a £340 discount, so the line says "£687.00 less £340.00 discount" and claims £347. That's what we actually paid.
  • Leave out £0 lines. The statement had an X-ray marked "plan patient" with no charge. I asked whether adding it would get us more money. It wouldn't: insurers refund what you paid, and claiming for something free is a false claim.
  • Re-read the list after every edit. At one point the portal quietly changed a line's type from Routine to Emergency while the agent was renaming it. The agent spotted it because it read the saved table back after every change, and it fixed it.
  • Watch out for characters the portal strips. Ours silently removed opening brackets, so "X-ray (intraoral)" was saved as "X-ray intraoral)". Hyphens worked fine.

Before you move on, check that the claim total exactly matches what you paid on the statement.

Step 5: Don't stretch the categories

Partway through, I asked the agent whether the second appointment could also go in as an emergency, since emergencies pay 100%. It said no, and quoted the policy back to me: the emergency benefit covers "treatment carried out at an emergency appointment which was not pre-planned" and excludes "any treatment carried out at a follow up appointment."

It also did the sums. Our restorative allowance was already used up by the main treatment, and the emergency benefit has its own cap, so moving the line would have gained us about £120 at most, and that was the sort of change that gets a whole claim queried. I was glad it said no. An agent that only does what you ask isn't enough. You want one that checks your request against the policy wording and pushes back.

Step 6: Uploading evidence: check the formats and save often

This step took the longest:

  • The original PDF was rejected as "potentially malicious content". Scanned PDFs often trip these filters. We used an image of the statement instead.
  • Then the portal looked as if it had accepted PNG files, but the Next button did nothing, with no error message. Further down, the page listed the accepted formats: JPEG, TIFF, BMP, PDF and Word. No PNG. The agent converted the files to JPG and the step went through.
  • The evidence was in an email, so the agent made a clean one-page image of the dentist's reply, including the sender, date and subject. Assessors want to see who said what, and when.
  • The agent's browser crashed once and we lost the unsaved draft. After that, it used the portal's "Save claim & return later" button after each major step. Most portals have one.

Step 7: The human submits

When it got to the bank details page, the agent stopped. That was what we'd agreed at the start, for two reasons:

  1. Bank details stay with me. I don't type my sort code and account number into the chat, for the same reason as the password.
  2. Submitting means signing a declaration that the claim is true. I'm the one making that declaration, so I should be the one pressing the button.

The agent saved the draft and sent me a summary: every line, the total, the two attached files, and what each portal page should show when I open it. I logged in, checked the four lines against the statement, added my bank details and pressed Submit. That took about two minutes.

The rules I'd keep

  1. The agent reads the policy and writes a plan with an expected refund before opening the portal.
  2. Passwords go in a vault, never in the chat. Bank details are entered by you.
  3. Two failed logins means stop and ask.
  4. Claim lines use the statement's exact wording, one per charged item. No £0 lines, nothing invented.
  5. The agent checks your requests against the policy wording and refuses anything that would make the claim untrue.
  6. Save the draft after every major step.
  7. The agent stops before Submit. You check and sign.

Total time from me: about 30 minutes over an evening, most of it spent saving the login and answering the agent's questions. The admin I'd normally put off until the deadline was finished in one sitting.

I'm a test automation engineer working on how to check AI systems properly, so I treated this partly as a test of where an agent needs hard limits. The answers were the password, the bank details, the declaration, and the honesty of the claim itself.
